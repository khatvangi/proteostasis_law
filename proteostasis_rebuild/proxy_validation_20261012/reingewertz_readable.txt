Abstract The Escherichia coli GroEL/ES chaperonin system facilitates protein folding in an ATP‐driven manner.
There are &lt;100 obligate clients of this system in E.
coli although GroEL can interact and assist the folding of a multitude of proteins in vitro .
It has remained unclear, however, which features distinguish obligate clients from all the other proteins in an E.
coli cell.
To address this question, we established a system for selecting mutations in mouse dihydrofolate reductase (mDHFR), a GroEL interactor, that diminish its dependence on GroEL for folding.
Strikingly, both synonymous and non‐synonymous codon substitutions were found to reduce mDHFR's dependence on GroEL.
The non‐synonymous substitutions increase the rate of spontaneous folding whereas computational analysis indicates that the synonymous substitutions appear to affect translation rates at specific sites.

Cortajarena 1 INTRODUCTION Owing to molecular crowding in cells, various mechanisms have evolved to ensure that functional protein–protein interactions can outcompete in vivo the much larger number of potential non‐functional interactions.
The need to avoid non‐specific interactions is also faced by molecular chaperones, which tend to bind their substrates in a relatively non‐specific manner from the chemical perspective.
Nevertheless, molecular chaperones must be able to discriminate in vivo between their obligate clients and the many other (in some cases similar) non‐client proteins found in the cell owing, for example, to their limited capacity.
Here, we explore how such specificity is achieved in vivo in the case of the Escherichia coli GroE molecular chaperone system, which assists protein folding in an ATP‐fueled manner (Hayer‐Hartl et al., 2016 ; Horovitz et al., 2022 ; Horwich &amp; Fenton, 2020 ; Thirumalai et al., 2020 ).
The GroE system comprises GroEL, which consists of two back‐to‐back stacked rings that are each made up of seven identical subunits (Braig et al., 1994 ), and GroES, which is also a homo‐heptameric ring.
ATP‐dependent binding of GroES to one (Xu et al., 1997 ) or both ends of GroEL (Fei et al., 2014 ) creates a compartment, sometimes termed an Anfinsen cage, in which protein substrates are sequestered from bulk solution.
It remains controversial whether protein substrate encapsulation in the GroE cavity prevents aggregation but has no effect on the folding process itself (Hofmann et al., 2010 ; Horwich et al., 2009 ), accelerates folding (Georgescauld et al., 2014 ) or retards it and is destabilizing (Koculi &amp; Thirumalai, 2021 ; Korobko et al., 2020 ).
Early work showed that the interaction of GroEL with protein substrates is very promiscuous in vitro as might be expected given that it binds them in their non‐native states (Viitanen et al., 1992 ).
A back‐of‐the‐envelope calculation indicated, however, that GroEL abundance in vivo under normal conditions is sufficient to assist the folding of only about 5% of the proteins in an E.
coli cell (Lorimer, 1996 ), thereby indicating that GroEL‐protein substrate interactions in vivo must display some specificity.
In subsequent work, a list of GroEL interactors in vivo was established by using mass spectrometry to identify proteins enriched in the cavities of complexes of GroEL with His 6 ‐tagged GroES after isolating them from lysed E.
coli cells using metal affinity chromatography (Kerner et al., 2005 ).
About 250 GroEL interactors were identified using this approach (Kerner et al., 2005 ) of which ~80 were classified as obligate clients.
In later studies, the list of obligate clients was curated based on whether the proteins aggregate or degrade upon GroEL depletion (Fujiwara et al., 2010 ) and extended using more sensitive proteomic analysis (Niwa et al., 2016 ).
Inspection of the list of obligate substrates revealed that it is enriched in TIM barrels (Kerner et al., 2005 ) but TIM barrels with very similar sequences and structures can, however, differ markedly in their GroEL dependence.
For example, the sequences of the TIM barrels dihydrodipicolinate synthase (DapA) and YagE, an aldolase involved in formation of 2‐keto‐3‐deoxy‐galactonate, are 27% identical and 46% similar and their structures can be superimposed on each other with about 1.5 Å RMS deviation, but DapA is an obligate substrate and YagE is not (Kerner et al., 2005 ).
Another proposal was that substrate proteins contain GroES mobile loop‐like sequences since polypeptide substrates compete with the mobile loops for binding to the same sites in GroEL (Stan et al., 2005 ) but GroEL‐dependent TIM barrels were not found to be enriched in this motif relative to TIM barrels that are not GroEL‐dependent (Azia et al., 2012 ).
Identifying the features that distinguish clients from non‐clients has, therefore, remained an important and unanswered question concerning GroEL's function.
A powerful approach for resolving properties that distinguish clients from non‐clients involves testing whether small perturbations, for example, single mutations, which have biophysical effects that can be quantified and interpreted, can increase or decrease GroEL dependence.
In one such study, it was found that the ortholog of the E.
coli obligate client MetK (Fujiwara et al., 2010 ; Kerner et al., 2005 ) in Ureaplasma urealyticum , which lacks the groE gene in its genome (Clark &amp; Tillier, 2010 ; Glass et al., 2000 ), can be converted into a GroEL‐dependent folder by single mutations that increase aggregation propensity (Ishimoto et al., 2014 ).
A green fluorescent protein (GFP) variant was also converted into a GroEL‐dependent folder by a single mutation which, in this case, was proposed to introduce an entropic trap in the protein's folding pathway (Sadat et al., 2020 ).
Single mutations in GFP were previously found (Bandyopadhyay et al., 2017 ) to not only increase but also decrease GroEL dependence by increasing or decreasing energetic frustration (Ferreiro et al., 2014 ), respectively, which is associated with kinetic traps in the protein's folding pathway.
In addition, it was shown that increasing GFP's contact order via circular permutation without changing its structure increases its GroEL dependence in vivo and in vitro , thereby indicating that global, for example, topological, and not just local features affect GroEL dependence (Bandyopadhyay et al., 2019 ).
Given that gain‐of‐function mutations are, in general, more difficult to obtain than those that cause loss‐of‐function, we devised a selection strategy for identifying such mutations in mouse dihydrofolate reductase (mDHFR) that decrease its GroEL dependence in vivo .
Remarkably, both synonymous and non‐synonymous codon substitutions were found to reduce mDHFR's dependence on GroEL.
The non‐synonymous substitutions increase the rate of spontaneous folding whereas computational analysis suggests that the synonymous substitutions appear to affect translation rates at specific sites.
2 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 2.1 In vivo selection for mDHFR mutants with decreased GroEL dependence A strategy for selecting mutants with decreased GroEL dependence was devised (Figure 1 ) using mDHFR as a model system since it is a GroEL interactor (Viitanen et al., 1991 ) (whereas E.
coli DHFR is not) and its activity is essential as it catalyzes the formation of tetrahydrofolate which is required for purine and thymidylate synthesis.
A library of mDHFR mutants was generated by error‐prone PCR under conditions designed to achieve a frequency of 0–4.5 mutations/kb.
The library was expressed in E.
coli MGM100 cells (McLennan &amp; Masters, 1998 ) in which GroEL/ES expression is under control of the pBAD promotor, that is, it is turned on in the presence of arabinose and off in the presence of glucose.
The E.
coli MGM100 cells expressing mDHFR were grown also in the presence of trimethoprim (TMP), which strongly inhibits E.
coli DHFR but not mDHFR (Groom et al., 1991 ; Schweitzer et al., 1990 ).

Colonies expressing mutant mDHFR (such as the one circled in red) that grew better on glucose than colonies expressing wild‐type mDHFR (such as the one circled in black) were selected.
Initial selection for E.

It may be seen that some colonies grew better and others worse (e.g.
those circled in green and orange, respectively) on glucose vs.
arabinose in comparison with colonies expressing wild‐type mDHFR (circled in pink).
About 900 clones that grew better on plates containing glucose vs.
arabinose, in comparison with clones expressing wild‐type mDHDR, were identified and the mDHFR gene in 184 of them was sequenced.
About 40 of the sequenced clones with unique single missense mutations in mDHFR were then grown in liquid medium containing antibiotics, TMP, diaminopimelic acid (DAP) and either glucose or arabinose (Figures 2b and S1 ).
Addition of DAP, which is synthesized by the GroEL obligate client DapA and is required for cell wall synthesis (McLennan &amp; Masters, 1998 ), prevents cell lysis when growth is in the presence of glucose.
It may be seen that cells expressing the mDHFR G18S mutant, for example, grow better in liquid medium containing glucose vs.
arabinose, compared to cells expressing wild‐type mDHFR (Figure 2b ).
Eleven of the 40 clones with a single missense mutation (the mutation sites are shown in Figure S2 ) showed significantly better growth in the presence of glucose compared to clones expressing wild‐type mDHFR (Figures 2b and S1 ) and were, thus, chosen for further study.
It should be noted that biphasic growth curves, as measured here and observed before, have been attributed to changes in the composition of the medium during growth such as the exhaustion of a key metabolite (Monod, 1949 ).
It is also noteworthy that cells continue to grow for some hours (Figure S1 ), in the presence of glucose, most likely because essential GroE‐dependent proteins are present in sufficient excess (as indicated by the observation that overexpression of DapA can alleviate the effect of GroEL depletion; McLennan &amp; Masters, 1998 ) and because GroEL levels halve every generation, that is, GroEL depletion is not immediate.

2.2 Kinetic analysis of folding of mDHFR mutants that facilitate E.
coli growth upon GroEL depletion Increased mDHFR folding efficiency is one possible mechanism by which the mutations in mDHFR confer E.
coli growth upon GroEL depletion.
The folding of wild‐type mDHFR and the eleven mutants found to confer E.
coli growth upon GroEL depletion was, therefore, studied by denaturing them in urea and then monitoring their refolding, in the presence of GroEL, by following the regain in their enzyme activity from the change in absorbance at 340 nm.
It may be seen that plots of the change in absorbance at 340 nm as a function of time consist of a pre‐steady‐state lag phase followed by a steady‐state linear phase (Figure 3a ).
Representative plots for wild‐type mDHFR (Figure 3a ) show that the lag phase becomes more pronounced when the concentration of GroEL increases since folding is inhibited and that the value of the slope of the linear phase decreases since more mDHFR is GroEL‐bound and, thus, not active.
Good fits of these plots could be obtained using Equation ( A9 ) as reflected in the random deviations of the residuals about zero (Figure 3a ).
The fits yield estimates for the values of the rate constant, λ 2 , associated with the lag phase and the slope, V, of the linear phase.

The interpretation of λ 2 was based on a kinetic model (Figure 4 ) in which the unfolded protein substrate mDHFR can fold spontaneously to its native state or bind to the chaperonin GroEL and form a complex from which it can dissociate either folded or not.
According to this model, the value of λ 2 should decrease in a hyperbolic fashion with increasing GroEL concentrations as described by Equation ( A8 ) in the Appendix A .
Such a decrease is indeed observed as shown, for example, for wild‐type mDHFR and the R55T mutant (Figure 3b ).
Equation ( A8 ) was used to fit plots of the value of λ 2 as a function of GroEL concentration for the different mutants.
The fits yield estimates of the values of the spontaneous folding rate constant, k 1 , the rate constant for folding in association with GroEL, k 4 , and the affinity constant of the unfolded mDHFR variant for GroEL, K (Table 1 ).
It may be seen that the value of k 1 for all the mutants is higher than that of wild‐type mDHFR although in a few cases the increase is small.
The increase in k 1 is found in most cases to be accompanied by an increase also in k 4 but there is a significant ( r = −0.688, p &lt; 0.02) inverse linear relationship between ln( k 1 ) and ln( K ) (Figure 5 ).
This inverse relationship reflects the kinetic competition between spontaneous folding and binding to GroEL.
It indicates that dependence on GroEL for folding can be relieved surprisingly easily through single mutations that accelerate spontaneous folding and, thus, also reduce the affinity for GroEL.

2.3 Enzymatic activity of mDHFR mutants that facilitate E.
coli growth upon GroEL depletion Refolding of urea‐denatured mDHFR variants was carried out in the presence of GroEL and different concentrations of dihydrofolic acid (DHF) at a fixed NADPH concentration of 50 μM.
Values of the steady‐state enzyme velocity, V , were determined from the slopes of the linear phases of the plots of the change in the absorbance at 340 nm as a function of time and corrected using a calibration curve (see Methods).
The corrected values of V display a hyperbolic dependence on DHF concentration (Figure S3 ) in support of the derivation in the Appendix A .
Plots of V as a function of DHF concentration were fitted to the Michaelis–Menten equation.
The values of k cat and K m of wild‐type mDHFR were found to be similar to those reported previously (Thillet et al., 1990 ).
The values of k cat and K m of the mutants are found to be similar to those of wild‐type mDHFR (Table 1 ), thereby indicating that the mutations did not relieve GroEL dependence by increasing enzymatic activity.
2.4 Silent mutations in the mDHFR gene facilitate E.
coli growth upon GroEL depletion Codon optimality is often quantified using two measures (Bahiri‐Elitzur &amp; Tuller, 2021 ).
One is the codon adaptation index (CAI) (Sharp &amp; Li, 1987 ) which assumes that codons that appear in highly expressed genes are more optimal.
The second measure is the tRNA adaptation index (tAI) (dos Reis et al., 2004 ) which assumes that translational efficiency is determined by (i) tRNA concentrations as reflected in tRNA gene copy numbers and (ii) the different codon affinities of tRNA due to possible wobble interactions.
Two synonymous codon substitutions were found that enable E.
coli growth when GroEL is depleted.
One substitution, CCG→CCA in the codon for P27, results in a less frequent/optimal codon in E.
coli that is recognized by less abundant tRNA (the respective changes in CAI and tAI are 1.000→0.362 and 0.165→0.125) and, thus, slows the ribosome.
Interestingly, P27 is preceded by P26 and it is known that prolines, in general, and pairs of prolines even more so, stall translation (Huter et al., 2017 ; Krafczyk et al., 2021 ; Sabi &amp; Tuller, 2015 ).
The two prolines tend to be conserved in mammalian DHFRs (Figure 6a–c ) but not in the bacterial versions of this protein.
It is possible that this pair was introduced by evolution to slow the ribosome and improve co‐translational folding.
This hypothesis is supported by the fact that this silent mutation causes further slowing of the ribosome at this site by decreasing the adaptation to the tRNA pool.
Both the less optimal codons (Figure S4 ) and the pair of prolines are, however, not found at the corresponding positions in DHFRs from mollicutes with or without GroEL.

The second substitution, CCA→CCG in the codon for P129, involves changes in CAI and tAI opposite to those above but decreases the local mRNA folding energy (determined by the number and strength of interactions between nucleotides (Goz &amp; Tuller, 2015 ; Perach et al., 2021 ; Zafrir &amp; Tuller, 2015 )) from −6.5 to −7.1 kcal mol −1 , that is, stabilizes its structure, thereby slowing the ribosome (Tuller et al., 2011 ).
Strikingly, we find evidence for selection for stronger mRNA folding in this region only for the mollicutes without GroEL (Figure 6d ).
These results support the hypothesis that stronger mRNA folding in this region is necessary for improving co‐translational folding in mollicutes with no GroEL and that this mutation mimics the “evolutionary solution” found in bacteria with no GroEL.
The synonymous substitutions at positions 27 and 129 both result in slightly higher expression levels of mDHFR (Figure S5 ).
This is consistent with changes in codon usage affecting protein folding yields and, thus, alleviating GroEL dependence, as indicated by the computational analyses.
Other synonymous codon substitutions were, however, also found to have such an effect (Figure S5 ).
Hence, a more systematic investigation of the impact of changes in codon usage is needed.
It is important to note that the interaction of DHFR with GroEL may be under selection pressure even in organisms in which it is a non‐client since DHFR may interact with GroEL transiently or under stress conditions.
Moreover, it has been suggested that GroEL may assist in the folding of a majority of newly translated polypeptides and not just those considered clients (Chapman et al., 2006 ).
3 CONCLUSIONS In this work, we selected for single mutants of mDHFR that facilitate E.
coli growth when GroEL is depleted.
Kinetic analysis of the refolding of these mutants in vitro , which was monitored by following the regain in their enzymatic activity, provided estimates of their spontaneous and GroEL‐associated folding rate constants, affinity for GroEL, k cat and K m .
Although various mechanisms can, in principle, account for how single missense mutations alleviate dependence on GroEL for folding, we find here that the only effect common to all the non‐silent mutants is an increase in the spontaneous folding rate constant.
This finding is in line with previous work that indicated that frustration affects GroEL dependence (Bandyopadhyay et al., 2017 ).
Interestingly, all the mutations except for I17N and D146N are also predicted (Rawat et al., 2020 ) to reduce aggregation.
In accordance with this prediction, inspection of the mutation sites in the structure (Figure S2 ) shows that most of them are in beta‐strands and, thus, with potential to reduce aggregation.
Surprisingly, two silent mutations in codons for proline were also found to alleviate GroEL dependence.
Although there is growing evidence that codon usage can affect protein folding and structure (Moss et al., 2024 ; O'Brien et al., 2014 ), experimental evidence showing its impact on GroEL dependence has not been reported before.
Interestingly, our analysis suggests that the mechanism of action of one of these mutations is via its effect on mRNA folding, i.e.
increasing mRNA folding strength can improve co‐translational folding.
This result supports the hypothesis that selection for stable mRNA structures in coding regions is at least partially related to efficient co‐translational protein folding (Peeri &amp; Tuller, 2020 ).
It is clear, however, that experimental data for testing the hypothesized mechanism(s) by which changes in codon usage can alleviate GroEL dependence are still missing.
Taken together, our results indicate that any factor that affects kinetic partitioning between folding and other processes such as mis‐folding and aggregation can impact GroEL dependence and that predictors of GroEL dependence will also need to consider codon usage.
An intriguing question raised by our work that remains open concerns the mechanisms responsible for clients not evolving to be non‐clients.
Lack of selection pressure, unappreciated selective advantages in being a client or barriers associated with co‐occurrence of several mutations are some possibilities and they may depend on the client identity.
4 METHODS 4.1 Molecular biology The gene for wild‐type mDHFR fused to a C‐terminal His 6 tag connected by a 6 residue (GSDLRS) linker in the pDS56 vector (Bujard et al., 1987 ) was amplified by PCR using the respective forward and reverse primers: 5′‐GAGCACTTCACCAACAAGGACCATAGCATATGGTTCGACCATTGAACTGC‐3′ and 5′‐TTAATCTGTATCAGGCTGAAAATCTTAGTGATGGTGATGGTGATG‐3′.
The purified PCR product was then used as a megaprimer to insert this gene into the pMALc2 plasmid by restriction‐free (RF) cloning (Unger et al., 2010 ).
Random mutations in the mDHFR gene were introduced by error‐prone PCR using the GeneMorph II random mutagenesis kit (Agilent Technologies, Cat# 200550) and the respective forward and reverse primers: 5′‐GACAATTAATCATCGGCTCGT‐3′ and 5′‐TCACTTCTGAGTTCGGCATGG‐3′.
The manufacturer's protocol for a low mutation frequency (0–4.5 mutations/kb) was followed.
pMALc2 plasmids containing the mutated genes were generated by RF cloning using the gel‐purified PCR products of the mutated genes as megaprimers.
Cloning of the wild‐type mDHFR gene into pET‐21a(+) for recombinant expression was done by amplification of this gene in pMALc2 using the respective forward and reverse primers: 5′‐TTTAAGAAGGAGATATACATATGGTTCGACCATTG‐3′ and 5′‐CGGATCTCAGTGGTGGTGGTGGTGGTGAGATCGAAGATC‐3′.
The gene was then inserted into pET‐21a(+) by RF cloning using the purified PCR product as a megaprimer.
Mutations in the gene for mDHFR in pET‐21a(+) were carried out by one‐step RF cloning (Erijman et al., 2014 ) using mutation‐specific forward primers and the reverse primer: 5′‐CGGGCTTTGTTAGCAGCCGGATCTCAGTGGTGGTGGTGGTGGTGAG‐3′.
4.2 Selection of mDHFR mutants with decreased GroEL dependence in vivo E.
coli MGM100 cells, in which the chromosomal GroEL and GroES genes are under the control of arabinose promoter (McLennan &amp; Masters, 1998 ), were transformed with pMALc2 containing the gene for wild‐type mDHFR or variants with the random mutations.
The cells were then spread on LB plates containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin, 50 μg/mL kanamycin and 0.2% arabinose (LB‐AKA) and grown overnight at 37°C.
Single colonies were picked and grown for 2.5 h at 37°C in 50 μL of LB‐AKA.
The cells were then spun and resuspended in LB containing only antibiotics four times in order to remove the arabinose.
The washed cells were resuspended in 25 μL LB containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 50 μg/mL kanamycin and 1.8 μL from each culture were then spotted at corresponding positions on LB agar plates containing either 0.2% arabinose or 0.2% glucose and 100 μg/mL ampicillin, 50 μg/mL kanamycin, 0.5 mM isopropyl‐β‐d‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and 10 μg/mL of the E.
coli DHFR inhibitor TMP (Groom et al., 1991 ; Schweitzer et al., 1990 ).
The plates were incubated overnight at 37°C and then moved to 4°C.

4.6 Bioinformatic analyses Databases .
The DHFR protein sequences of 13 mollicute species containing GroEL (GroEL + ) and 20 species lacking GroEL (GroEL − ) (Schwarz et al., 2018 ) were downloaded from EMBL‐EBI UniProt database ( https://www.uniprot.org/ ), based on their accession ID.
The proteins were then mapped to their nucleotide level accession ID, using a dedicated python script, and all the nucleotide sequences were downloaded in a fasta format.
The sequences were divided into two groups (GroEL + and GroEL − ) and the WT (wild‐type) mDHFR gene sequence was added (first 558 nucleotides).
A multiple sequence alignment (MSA) file was generated for each group using the Clustal Omega tool (Sievers &amp; Higgins, 2018 ).
The bacterial genomes and their corresponding protein coding sequences were downloaded from the NIH GenBank database ( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/ ) (Benson et al., 2013 ).
Randomization models .
To evaluate our hypotheses of evolutionary selection, we employed two null models (for each MSA) that generate an output nucleotide sequence by preserving certain known features in the input sequence while randomly altering others.
In one model, vertical codon usage bias (VCUB), synonymous codons in a column in the MSA are permuted, thus, not affecting the MSA codon frequencies.
In the second model (COL_PERM), columns in the MSA are permuted (only synonymous codons are replaced) thus preserving the evolutionary distance between the genes/sequences in the MSA.
For each sequence, we generated 1000 VCUB‐based randomizations and 1000 COL_PERM‐based randomizations.
Models and measures for translation efficiency and mRNA folding stability .
Two types of measures were used to analyze codon usage.
One is the codon adaptation index (CAI) (Sharp &amp; Li, 1987 ) which assumes that codons that appear in highly expressed genes are more optimal.
The calculation is done for the top 15% most highly expressed genes using the directional codon bias score as a proxy for expression level (Sabi &amp; Tuller, 2014 ).
The second measure is the tRNA adaptation index (tAI) (dos Reis et al., 2004 ) which assumes that translational efficiency is determined by (i) tRNA concentrations as reflected in tRNA gene copy numbers and (ii) the different codon affinities of tRNA due to possible wobble interactions.
The tRNA copy numbers were taken from previous work (Chan &amp; Lowe, 2009 ) or were calculated using the tRNAscan‐SE‐2.0 software tool (Lowe &amp; Chan, 2016 ).
The CAI and tAI scores were calculated as described before (Tuller et al., 2010 ).
In addition, all the weights were normalized to generate consistent profiles.
Normalization was carried out by (i) dividing each weight vector by its own standard deviation; and (ii) subtracting the overall average from each column such that each weight vector now has the same average and a standard deviation of 1.
We adjusted all vectors to the [0,1] interval, i.e.
divided all of them by the maximum value.
Based on the CAI and tAI measures and mRNA folding analysis we built selection profiles for the MSAs and randomization models.
We looked for positions with a significant selection for fast/slow codons (both VCUB and COL_PERM randomization models; p 1 , p 2 &gt;0.05).
The folding selection analysis is based on our algorithm for detecting selection for strong or weak mRNA local folding energy (LFE) (Goz &amp; Tuller, 2015 ; Perach et al., 2021 ; Zafrir &amp; Tuller, 2015 ).
We looked for positions with significant evidence for selection for high/low local LFE (both VCUB and COL_PERM randomization models; Z‐score &gt;2; Z‐score &lt;−2).
LFE window size is 39 nucleotides.
