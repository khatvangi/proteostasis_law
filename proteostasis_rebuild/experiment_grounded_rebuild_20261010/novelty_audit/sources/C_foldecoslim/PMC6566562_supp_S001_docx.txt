Parametrizing the Background Proteome

Modeling the background proteome requires estimates of key parameters for as many proteins as possible, including abundances, folding and aggregation rate constants, and competence for chaperoning. New mass spectrometry-based methods have enabled such proteome-wide estimates to be made for protein stabilities in a number of systems, including E. coli.^(2,3) These measurements of folding equilibrium constants (K_(f,i)) can be combined with estimates of protein folding rate constants (k_(f,i)) to calculate unfolding rate constants (k_(u,i); since k_(u,i) = k_(f,i)/K_(f,i)). The necessary folding rate constants are not available from direct experimental measurements, but it has been shown that the topologies of protein structures explain much of the variability in folding rate constants, so this information can be used to predict folding rate constants for proteins whose structure is known.⁴⁻⁸ Thus, folding rate constants were estimated as described previously⁸ and used in combination with folding equilibrium constants to calculate unfolding rate constants for the hundreds of E. coli proteins for which X-ray crystal structures have been reported. For multi-domain proteins, the rate constant for the slowest folding domain was used, since this would likely be the rate limiting step in the overall folding of such a protein.

With estimates of k_(f,i) and k_(u,i) in hand, proteome-wide solubility measurements made in cell-free translation systems by Niwa and co-workers⁹ can be used to estimate rate constants for aggregation, k_(agg,i). Since these solubility measurements were made in the absence of chaperones and proteases, FoldEcoSlim can be used with all parameters set to 0 except k_(f,i) and k_(u,i) and the synthesis rate (σ_(i), which can be determined from the total amount of protein synthesized in the experiment) and solved for the value of k_(agg,i) that reproduces the observed extent of aggregation. A complete set of estimates for k_(f,i), k_(u,i), and k_(agg,i) were obtained in this way for 640 proteins representing 37% of the E. coli proteome by mass.

The aptitude for chaperoning was measured for many of the less soluble proteins examined by Niwa and co-workers by adding the DnaK/DnaJ/GrpE or GroEL/GroES systems to the same cell-free translation system and measuring the increase in solubility.¹⁰ Given the wide distribution of DnaK binding sites in the E. coli proteome (on average, each protein has over 20),¹¹ we assumed that the rate constant for the initial binding step (k_(K,in)) is substrate independent, as we^(1,12) and others^(13,14) have done previously. Thus, we account for a protein’s aptitude for chaperoning by K entirely with the parameter k_(K,disagg,i), the rate constant for conversion of K:A_(i) to K:U_(i). Using the estimates of k_(f,i), k_(u,i), and k_(agg,i) obtained as described above, k_(K,disagg,i) was varied to find the value that reproduced the observed increase in solubility in the presence of the DnaK/DnaJ/GrpE system. In contrast to the DnaK/DnaJ/GrpE system, the GroEL/GroES system seems to be more selective in its substrates. This assertion is consistent with the observation that only ~250 proteins co-immunoprecipitate with GroEL¹⁵ (of which ~60 are obligate substrates¹⁶). Thus, we varied the rate constant for substrates entering the GroEL/GroES cycle (k_(G,in,i)) to find the value that reproduced the observed increase in solubility in the presence of the GroEL/GroES system. There were 120 proteins for which estimates of k_(f,i), k_(u,i), and k_(agg,i) as well as measurements of chaperone efficacy were available. The remaining proteins were well behaved in the cell free translation system,^(9,10) and therefore unlikely to be substantial contributors to the burden on the proteostasis network. For modeling purposes, they were assigned the median value of the estimates for k_(K,disagg,i) (0.0037 s⁻¹) and a minimal value for k_(G,in,i) (10⁻³ M⁻¹ s⁻¹).

Individually tracking each of the 640 proteins for which full parameter sets were available would be excessively computationally expensive, even in a relatively simple model like FoldEcoSlim. We therefore coarse-grained the proteome by clustering the proteins into 16 groups based on the similarities between their parameters. The clusters were assigned the abundance-weighted mean parameters of their members. The fractional abundance of the clusters was calculated by dividing the sum of the abundances of the cluster members (as determined by mass spectrometry by Schmidt and co-workers for E. coli growing in LB media¹⁷) by the sum of the abundances of all the proteins in the dataset. One cluster (cluster 16) was set aside to contain the obligate GroEL substrates, as defined by Fujiwara and co-workers.¹⁶

The resulting proteome produced an unrealistically high amount of protein aggregation (~12% of the proteome). However, there are chaperone systems in E. coli that suppress aggregation in vivo that are not included in FoldEcoSlim (for example, ClpB¹⁸ and IbpA/IbpB¹⁹). In addition, it is possible that the cytosolic environment is more solubilizing for proteins than the environment of the in vitro translation system in which the solubilities were originally measured. To account for these factors, we found that a decrease in the aggregation rate constants (by a factor of about 7) and an increase in the efficiencies of the DnaK/DnaJ/GrpE cycle (by a factor of about 3) across the proteome mitigated the excessive aggregation that we observed. The final protein-dependent parameters for the protein clusters are displayed in Figure 2 and are listed in Table S1. The un-adjusted parameters for individual proteins are listed in Table S2.

The Contribution of the GroEL/GroES System to Proteostasis Depends on the Relative Rates of Substrate Folding and Release

In terms of the pseudo-state sum, the GroEL/GroES system influences proteostasis primarily through Q_(N,i) and the DnaK/DnaJ/GrpE system primarily through Q_(A,i) (both systems contribute to Q_(B,i), but this is not a major contributor to Z_(SS,i) under normal conditions). The assistance that the GroEL/GroES system provides to protein folding is expressed in the term Γ_(i) in the expression for Q_(N,i)

[]

For simplicity, we will assume in the analysis that follows that folding for proteins of interest is not enhanced by the GroEL cavity (γ_(i) = 1), and we will ignore the factor γ_(i) hereafter. The magnitude of Γ_(i) depends on the product of three factors, the first of which is k_(G,out) / (k_(u,i) + λ). This factor represents the relative rate of release of natively folded protein from the GroEL cavity compared to the rate of unfolding of native protein. The native state can only accumulate substantially from GroEL-assisted folding if k_(G,out) > (k_(u,i) + λ); if this relation is not true, the protein will unfold faster than it is released from GroEL.

The second factor is k_(f,i) / (k_(f,i) + k_(u,i) + k_(G,out) + λ). This factor is equal to the fraction of the GroEL-bound protein that is in the native state and it approaches its maximum of 1 when the intra-cavity folding rate constant (k_(f,i)) is much faster than unfolding (k_(u,i)), release from the cavity (k_(G,out)) and cell division (λ). It decreases from 1 if either k_(u,i) or k_(G,out) approaches or exceeds k_(f,i). The former condition would indicate native state instability. The latter, more likely condition would indicate that folding cannot reach completion on the time scale of the ATP-driven GroEL/GroES conformational cycle.

The last factor is k_(G,in,i) [G] / (k_(G,out) + λ). In the numerator, the free concentration of G is largely determined by the folding load exerted by the proteome as a whole and is not strongly influenced by the properties of any individual substrate (as long as that substrate’s expression level is not too high). Thus, in the analysis of the effects of mutations on a low-to-moderately expressed individual protein, [G] will be roughly constant and k_(G,in,i) [G] can be treated as a pseudo-first order rate constant which we will denote k’_(G,in,i). In the denominator, k_(G,out) is set by the ATP hydrolysis rate of the GroEL/GroES complex (~0.1 s⁻¹),²⁰ which is two hundred-fold greater than λ even for very rapidly dividing cells, so k_(G,out) + λ ≈ k_(G,out). Given these approximations, the third factor becomes k’_(G,in,i) / k_(G,out), which is essentially the substrate binding equilibrium constant for the GroEL/GroES system.

Again, the factor in parenthesis, 1 + k’_(G,in,i)/k_(G,out) represents the increase in Q_(N,i) due to the GroEL/GroES system. This result indicates that the effect of the GroEL/GroES system is simply proportional to its ability to bind the substrate. In this regime, the GroEL/GroES system provides a parallel channel for folding the protein of interest, but in an environment in which the protein is safeguarded from aggregation and degradation. Comparing equations and , it is clear that the effect of GroEL on proteostasis is greater for proteins that fold slowly. For a protein with a given ratio of k_(f,i)/k_(u,i)—that is, with a given thermodynamic stability—that falls into the fast-folding regime described above (k_(f,i) >> k_(u,i) + k_(G,out) + λ) the value of Q_(N,i) will increase as k_(f,i) decreases (recall that k_(u,i) also decreases in this scenario to maintain a constant K_(f,i)). At first this will be because the GroEL/GroES multiplier for the protein, the quantity 1 + k_(G,in,i)/k_(f,i), increases. As k_(f,i) continues to decrease, however, the protein will eventually cross over from the fast- to the slow-folding regime and the multiplier will asymptotically approach 1 + k_(G,in,i)/k_(G,out). This behavior is observed in the plot of f_(N,i) vs. Δln K_(f,i) and φ for cluster 7 in Figure 3. The curvatures of the contours at higher φ-values indicate that highly destabilizing mutations are less deleterious when they primarily diminish the folding rate. Thus, the GroEL/GroES system inverts the usual effects of mutations.

The DnaK/DnaJ/GrpE System Is the Primary Means of Suppressing Aggregation Even in Fast-Dividing Bacteria

The DnaK/DnaJ/GrpE system influences proteostasis by dispersing aggregates. It can also bind to unfolded proteins, but, rather than helping them to fold, it tends to release them in a state that is still unfolded (if anything, it is an even more expanded state²¹⁻²³). Thus, among the steady state constants in the pseudo-state sum of a given protein, the DnaK/DnaJ/GrpE system contributes to Q_(A,i) (for aggregates) and Q_(B,i) (for chaperone-bound proteins). As noted above, Q_(B,i) rarely makes substantial contributions to Z_(SS,i); thus, we will focus on Q_(A,i).

The latter cluster represents proteins that are very inefficiently disaggregated by the DnaK/DnaJ/GrpE system, but these proteins are, probably not coincidentally, the favored substrates of the GroEL/GroES system.

Protein Degradation Can Suppress Aggregation, but Suppresses Native Folding Equally

Proteases like Lon in E. coli dispose of proteins that are not folded and are therefore vulnerable to misfolding and aggregation. The extent of degradation is determined by the steady state constant Q_(D,i), which is defined in Equation (5) in the main text. In FoldEcoSlim, the value of Q_(D,i) is largely protein independent. The free concentration of D, which is in the numerator of the expression for Q_(D,i), should be unaffected by mutations in proteins that are not abundant. Thus, we can set [D] = 73 nM. (Recall that, as discussed above, the parameters for degradation were adjusted to match the observed rate of protein turnover in E. coli.^(24,25)) Given this value of Q_(D,i), a substantial fraction of any protein with a value of Q_(N,i) that is not much greater than 9.17 will be degraded. Because of this, mutations that decrease Q_(N,i) can lead to excessive degradation and loss of function. In addition, it is important to note that manipulations of the proteostasis network that increase the levels of general proteases like Lon will succeed in decreasing protein aggregation because Q_(D,i) is in the denominator of f_(A,i) (see Equation (3)) However, Q_(D,i) is also in the denominator of f_(N,I) (see Equation (2)), so increasing Q_(D,i) will also decrease f_(N,i). The former goal cannot be achieved by increasing proteolysis without the latter consequence. For a degradation system to be able to selectively decrease aggregation, it must directly degrade aggregates as the eukaryotic autophagy system does.²⁶

For Y133S MmCRABP1, k_(N-I2) is in Table 1 of Budyak and co-workers²⁷ and k_(I2-N) is reported by Pobre³⁵ (see Table 2.1A of that work). The value of k_(I1-I2) is not directly reported, but since the values of k_(I1-I2) and k_(I2-N) reported by Budyak and co-workers²⁷ are correlated, consistent with the transition states having similar topologies (ln k_(I1-I2) = 1.91 + 1.12 k_(I2-N); R² = 0.54; data not shown), the value of k_(I1-I2) for Y133S MmCRABP1 can be estimated from its value of k_(I2-N). This estimate for k_(I1-I2) for Y133S MmCRABP1 is given in Table 1 of this work.
