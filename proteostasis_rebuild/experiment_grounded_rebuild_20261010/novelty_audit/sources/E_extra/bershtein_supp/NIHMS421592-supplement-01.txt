Supplemental Experimental Procedures

Selection of mutants and the conditions for the study
To determine the role of folding stability, we had to restrict our choice of mutations to those that would have minimal effect on the enzymatic activity. Using structural and sequence analysis, we selected 10 loci, most buried in the hydrophobic core of the C-terminal part of the protein, located at least 4 Å away from the substrate and cofactor binding sites. Out of the 16 single mutant DHFR proteins generated, 13 were found slightly to severely destabilized (with apparent G values ranging between 0.18 to 2.87 kcal/mol) with only a mild change in the catalytic activity (up to 2-3 fold in the  kcat/KM values for most mutants) . To further increase the destabilization, 10 multiple DHFR mutants were created by combining 4 most destabilizing single substitutions. We were able to express and purify 19 out of total of 26 DHFR mutants for the in vitro analysis. Next, each mutant folA gene was incorporated at its endogenous locus in the MG1655 E. coli chromosome without perturbing the integrity of the gene’s regulatory region (see Supplemental Experimental Procedures below and ), thus providing total of 26 mutant E. coli strains carrying specific mutations in the folA gene. The effect of the endogenous folA mutations on fitness was determined by measuring growth rates of each mutant at 300C, 370C, and 420C (see Experimental Procedures, and Table S1). Being a gut bacteria, E. coli inhabit environments with very different temperature profiles that shift from a host core body temperature (37-38oC for mammals, 40-45oC for birds)  to water sediments (ambient temperature). In our view, it is more relevant, therefore, to consider a range of temperatures with regard to fitness effects, rather than following a single conventional temperature (37oC).  This view is consistent with the “Red Queen effect” hypothesis in protein homeostasis that states that quality control machinery should be switched on at all times (and not only at the elevated temperatures, or other stressful conditions) to sustain foldable (functional) proteome . We found that for the MG1655 E. coli strain used in our experiments, the temperature dependence of growth rates follows the Arrhenius law ) in the range of 30-42oC, with maximal (optimal) growth rate reached at 42oC (Figure S7). Arrhenius temperature dependence of the growth rates indicates that metabolic activity is the main limiting factor that determines organismal fitness under the given conditions .
 
 

Site-directed chromosomal mutagenesis of the folA gene
The method is described at greater length in our recent publication  Briefly, the folA gene carrying the desired mutation(s) with an entire endogenous regulatory region  (191 bp separating the stop codon of the upstream kefC gene and the start codon of folA gene) was ligated into a pKD13 plasmid flanked by two different antibiotic markers (genes encoding for kanamycin (kanR) and chloramphenicol (cmR) resistances). The entire cassette was then amplified with two primers tailed with 50 nucleotides homologous to the chromosomal region intended for recombination (kefC gene upstream and apaH gene downstream of folA). The amplified product was transformed into BW25113 strain with induced Red helper plasmids, and the recombinants selected on plates carrying both antibiotics. Strains carrying the desired mutation in the chromosome were verified by sequencing. Identified chromosomal mutations were then moved to MG1655 strain by P1 transduction and double antibiotic selection (kan and cm) and again verified by sequencing. 

Protein expression and purification
The folA genes carrying the mutations and a C-terminal His-tag were cloned in pET24 expression vector under an inducible T7 promoter, transformed into BL21(DE3) cells and expressed by a standard protocol. The recombinant proteins were purified on Ni-NTA columns (Qiagen) to over 95% purity.

Measuring the intracellular protein abundance
Cells were grown in supplemented M9 medium for 6 hours at 30oC, chilled on ice for 30 min and lysed with BugBuster (Novagen). The insoluble fraction was separated by centrifugation, solubilized by Inclusion Body Solubilization Reagent (Thermo Scientific), and dialyzed against PBS. The overall amount of protein in the lysates was estimated by the BCA protein assay kit (Pierce). DHFR amounts in the soluble and insoluble fractions were determined by SDS-PAGE followed by Western Blot using Rabbit-anti E.coli’s DHFR polyclonal antibodies (custom raised by Pacific Immunology) by measuring densities of the DHFR bands.
 
Estimating the aggregation propensity
The aggregation propensity in a range of temperatures was determined in vitro by thermal unfolding of the DHFR proteins in the presence of Proteostat aggregation dye (Enzo Life Sciences) by a fluorescence signal. To this end, 2   of a DHFR protein (10 mM K-phoshate buffer pH8.2, 0.2 mM EDTA, 1 mM beta-mercaptoethanol, 750  NADPH) in the presence of 3  Proteostat dye were heated from 25oC to 70oC  at 0.2oC/min. The fluorescence signal that emanated from the dye was monitored (excitation 550 nm, emission 600 nm) (Cary-eclipse fluorescence spectrophotometer, Varian). 

Enzymatic activity
Initial velocity V0 of the DHFR catalyzed reaction was measured by following the linear drop in NADPH absorbance at 340 nm (mOD/min). To this end, purified enzymes (30 nM) were pre-incubated with 120   NADPH in MTEN buffer (50 mM 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid, 25 mM tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane, 25mM ethanolamine, and 100 mM sodium chloride, pH7) for 15 min at room temperature.
