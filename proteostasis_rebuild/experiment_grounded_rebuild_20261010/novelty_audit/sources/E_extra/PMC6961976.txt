The holdase paradigm of chaperone action involves preferential binding by the chaperone to the unfolded protein state, thereby preventing it from either, associating with other unstable proteins (to form large dysfunctional aggregates), or being degraded by the proteolytic machinery of the cell/organism. In this paper we examine the necessary physical constraints imposed upon the holdase chaperone response in a cell-like environment and use these limitations to comment on the likely nature of the optimal form of chaperone response in vivo. pmc-status-qastatus 0 pmc-status-live yes pmc-status-embargo no pmc-status-released yes pmc-prop-open-access no pmc-prop-olf no pmc-prop-manuscript yes pmc-prop-open-manuscript yes pmc-prop-legally-suppressed no pmc-prop-has-pdf yes pmc-prop-has-supplement yes pmc-prop-pdf-only no pmc-prop-suppress-copyright no pmc-prop-is-real-version no pmc-prop-is-scanned-article no pmc-prop-preprint no pmc-prop-in-epmc yes Introduction As the enzymatic and regulatory molecular workhorses of the cell, proteins make possible the chemistry of life. In carrying out their normal functions, linear polypeptide chains adopt a low-energy conformational state, known as the native state, that possesses the required spatial arrangement of constituent functional groups to allow it to function as either a chemical catalyst, structural component or regulatory ligand [ 1 – 3 ]. The imposition of chemical or physical stress can distort the balance of forces generating the native structure(s) and cause the protein to adopt any number of alternative conformations, collectively termed the unfolded state [ 4 , 5 ]. During a period of stress the unfolded protein can undergo unwanted side-reactions leading to their aggregation, chemical modification and/or breakdown, which cause irreversible loss of function [ 6 ]. To combat this loss of valuable material the cell has developed an additional complement of proteins, termed the ‘molecular chaperones’ that act to prevent unfolded proteins from following such unproductive pathways [ 7 , 8 ]. Due to their historical association with the suite of proteins produced in response to a heat shock, the molecular chaperones found in eukaryotes follow a naming convention that combines the acronym for heat shock protein (Hsp) with the approximate protein molecular weight 1 [ 10 ]. As defined by Sharma et al. [ 11 ] there are five 2 major sequence conserved chaperone families (i) Hsp 100, (ii) Hsp90, (iii) Hsp70, (iv) Hsp60 and (v) sHsps (small heat shock proteins). The molecular mechanism underlying the chaperone action of these five families may be divided into two broad classes, the active foldases – which refold proteins through concerted physical action of the chaperone driven by ATP hydrolysis [ 12 ], and the passive holdases – which act via reversible complex formation, and are not associated with the breakage of any exergonic covalent bonds [ 13 , 14 ]. Of the five types described, only the small heat shock proteins (sHsps) are obligate holdase chaperones, with the other four groups displaying a mixture of holdase, foldase and disaggregase behaviors based on unique mechanisms [ 8 ]. Although many studies indicate an extreme regulatory complexity associated with the holdase mechanism of chaperone action [ 15 – 17 ] it is, nevertheless, the easiest type of chaperone action to attack from a modelling perspective, due to its potential for description within the time independent domain 3 . However, whilst the broad features of the holdase chaperone response are believed known, many specific details remain obscure. Indeed, despite the growing number of putative holdase-type chaperones [e.g. 19 , 20 ] questions relating to achieving a more defined chemical understanding (such as those pertaining to reaction mechanism, substrate specificity and reaction order) are often left unanswered or frequently not posed 4 . To combat a shortage of available formalism 5 here we explore methods for structuring and ordering the argument, and in the process, examine limitations to any chaperone response imposed by physical and chemical restraints associated with mass-action principles. The approach developed here aims to frame the argument in a manner that is capable of incorporating data as well as providing a launching pad for future hypothesis generation and experimental testing. Determination of an optimum chaperone response To evaluate the most beneficial chaperone response clearly two features are required, a target function that defines an optimum, and a set of parameters that can be methodically varied to enable system optimization to be carried out [ 34 ]. With regard to the development of a target function reflecting a system optimum, we note that chaperones have the potential to play a number of roles in maintaining cellular viability – we will focus on three. Proper protein function, whether it be as an enzyme, structural ligand or structural component, is dependent upon the polypeptide chain adopting a native folded structure 8 . Thus a simple-direct measure of optimal performance of the complement of proteins within the cell would be the total concentration of all proteins present in folded form, ( C F ) T O T [ 1 – 3 ]. Unregulated interaction of unfolded proteins can produce either amorphous aggregates or structured aggregates, known as amyloids [ 6 , 36 ]. Both types of aggregates can possess ‘sticky’ denaturing/adsorptive interfaces that may be potentially injurious to the cell through action as accumulation (adsorptive) sinks or via interaction with membranes [ 37 , 38 ]. As aggregation is a second-order 9 concentration dependent phenomenon it is highly sensitive to the concentration of unfolded protein. Thus the total concentration of unfolded protein ( C U ) T O T would be another measure likely to factor into the system optimum. The holdase-chaperone paradigm suggests that the chaperone complexed form of an unfolded protein is ‘safe’ relative to the unfolded (and perhaps folded) forms during a period of stress such as heat shock [ 6 ]. Given this point of view, the effective carrying capacity of the holdase-chaperone complement, reflected by the total concentration of the chaperone-unfolded protein complex, ( C C U ) T O T , may also be an important determinant of any system optimum. Discussion The chaperone concept holds great promise for deepening our understanding of protein folding in vitro [ 26 , 54 ] and in vivo [ 55 , 56 ] and we begin here with a brief history of the molecular chaperone field, to allow the current work to be placed in proper context. This chaperone mini-history is followed by a brief restatement of action of the present study (i.e. what was done in the current work). With this foundation as discussion ‘prologue’ I then describe the major findings of this study, with this section directly followed by another discussing the limitations of the simulation methodology and the assumptions on which it is based. Short history of molecular chaperones Current understanding sees the set of molecular chaperone proteins in partial overlap with the set of heat shock proteins (Hsps) 11 , 12 [ 23 , 60 ]. The heat shock response was first noted approximately 60 years ago by Ritossa based on serendipitous autoradiography-based observations of the overproduction of mRNA in the fruit fly ( Drosophila melanogaster) in response to accidental high temperature incubation [ 61 ]. Using [ 35 S]-methionine pulse chase experiments with the same fruit fly model, this heat induced buildup of mRNA was shown [ 62 ] to translate to an increase in six particular protein bands of approximate molecular weight 20, 25, 60, 70, 90 and 100 kDa, with these over-expressed proteins collectively constituting ~ 30% of the total protein content of the cell (after heat shock). These initial discoveries were followed by a period marked by advances made along several unconnected lines of investigation into individual proteins that were slowly identified and related with those in the heat shock complement. A consensus began to develop in the 1980s that the proteins produced in the heat shock response constituted a protective mechanism to prevent cell death under conditions of stress and that certain members of the heat shock protein group were found to be involved in limiting protein denaturation and aggregation [ 12 , 63 , 64 ]. Due to this role in preventing unwanted illicit protein behavior, they were given the name molecular chaperones [ 65 , 66 ]. In the mid to late 80s, the field coalesced under the general banner of chaperone biology and during this time five non-sequence related families of chaperones were recognized [ 67 ] ( Table 1 ) 13 , (i) Hsp 100 (ClpB) (M w > 100kDa), (ii) Hsp90 (HtpG) (M w 81 to 99kDa), (iii) Hsp70 (DnaK) (M w 65 to 80kDa), (iv) Hsp60 (GroEL) (M w 55 to 64kDa) and (v) sHsps (IbpA/B) (M w ≤ 34kDa). Upon mechanistic analysis. the chaperone families were subsequently further classified as active or passive, dependent on whether or not they hydrolyzed ATP in carrying out their normal function [ 8 ]. Within the ‘active’ chaperone class a number of distinct chaperone roles were identified. These roles include foldase/unfoldase activity [ 12 , 64 ] – in which the unfolding reaction is effectively catalyzed by the chaperone, and a dis-aggregase activity – in which small or large protein aggregates are broken apart [ 74 ]. The alternative ‘passive’ designation was given to the holdase-type chaperones [ 13 , 14 , 75 ] (as described in the current paper). Investigation into the functional form of the active chaperones has revealed amazingly complex bio-nanomachinery [ 76 – 79 ] the functional form of the holdase chaperone is thought to be at the level of monomer, dimer or small oligomers [ 79 , 80 ]. Table 1 presents a brief synopsis of the function of each chaperone class with the relevant background literature. As the field matured, additional classes of regulatory proteins such as Hsp40, NEFs, Hsp110 were designated as co-chaperones, due to their ability to bind to chaperones and modulate their activity [ 81 , 82 ]. Whilst practically all of the active chaperones necessarily also possess a passive holdase function (to initially set the unfolded protein in place) only the small heat shock proteins are obligate holdases of the type described in this paper – so it is worthwhile to address this group in a little greater detail than that provided in Table 1 . sHSPs are designated as ‘holdases’ [ 17 , 83 ] based on their ability to prevent the formation of protein aggregates in vitro . Found in all forms of life across the bacteria, archaea and eukarya domains, the sHsps are truly ubiquitous with humans possessing 10 sHsps, the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae having 2 sHsps and the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans having 18 sHsps [ 10 ]. The sHsp protein structure is characterized by three domains comprised of variable length unstructured N and C terminal domains and a highly conserved central alpha-crystallin domain [ 84 ]. Most sHsps are known to exist in a dynamic monomer/dimer/oligomer reversible equilibrium (higher quaternary forms present as poly-disperse oligomers made up of between 12 and 48 monomers [ 31 , 84 – 86 ] with the active state believed to frequently involve the monomeric (or low oligomer) form [ 79 , 87 ]. Brief restatement of action (sans equations) This simulation study initially posited the existence of two distinct pools of protein – the soluble folded monomeric chaperone and the soluble monomeric protein complement capable of undergoing reversible two state folding. The soluble two-folding state protein pool was considered to consist of (N P ) TOT different proteins (set at 10,000) [ 22 , 88 ]. The total summed number concentration of this unfoldable protein complement, (C P ) TOT , was set at 10mM and the form of their relational concentration distribution was set as an exponential ( Eqn. 1 ). These values were chosen on the basis that, for an average protein size of 20 kDa, this would make for a cytosolic protein weight concentration of 200 mg/ml – within the range of that experimentally recorded for prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells [ 89 , 90 ]. All of the (N P ) TOT proteins were assumed to follow a two-state thermal unfolding mechanism, meaning that each protein could transition between folded and unfolded states. The value of the equilibrium unfolding constant governing each protein’s respective unfolding transition at a particular temperature, K F i U i ( T ) , was calculated on the basis of three parameters, T m i , Δ h F i U i ∘ and Δ σ F i U i , respectively defining their temperature transition midpoint, molar enthalpy difference of unfolding and molar heat capacity difference. Each protein’s T m i , Δ h F i U i ∘ and Δ σ F i U i were respectively assigned by sampling from a specific renormalized truncated Gaussian distribution ( Eqn. 3 ). The holdase-chaperone pool was postulated to possess the following properties, (i) The holdase-chaperone system constituted either 0 or 30% of the total number concentration of all proteins in the cell including chaperones [this made (C C ) tot = 0 or 0.42((C P ) tot ]. (ii) The holdase-chaperone protein pool was composed of (N C ) TOT discrete chaperone sub-types (set at 10), with each discrete type characterized by a non-specific affinity for unfolded protein, binding with 1:1 stoichiometry and assigned an affinity on a scale of log 10 ( K UCi ) from 2 to 11. (iii) Each j th sub-type of the holdase chaperone pool was assigned a total concentration, ( C C j ) tot , constrained by the fixed total amount of chaperones and a chaperone concentration distribution specified by a truncated Gaussian ( Eqn. 7 ). Interaction between the chaperone and non-chaperone protein components was calculated using a macroscopic model based on equilibrium mass action relationships for a well-mixed solution. Major findings of the simulation study Perhaps the most striking result of this study was the demonstration that, for a single temperature condition, ‘better’ holdase-chaperone distributions (i.e. chaperones possessing higher binding affinity and greater concentration) actually lead to a significant decrease in the folded concentration of protein ( Fig. 5 – top row) by dragging the equilibrium towards the complexed state ( Fig. 9a ). Given that the folded form of the protein is usually the active functional form, such a simple, but important, finding implies a potential hazard to any cell under non-stressed conditions and necessitates tight control of the chaperone response (the mechanistic form of this control will be discussed in the next section). With regard to the general point of regulation, Fig. 6 explores the phenomenon of ‘tuning’ the chaperone distribution at a constant temperature. Dramatic changes in species concentration can be achieved by altering the chaperone concentration and/or its affinity for unfolded protein. Fig. 7 describes the classical case of chaperone related heat shock response – in which chaperone extent is increased following a period of exposure to high temperature [ 23 , 24 , 62 , 91 , 92 ]. This mode of chaperone production leads to a net decrease in the total concentration of folded protein, and a net increase in the total concentrations of free, and chaperone complexed, unfolded protein. As noted in Fig.7 , higher affinity forms of the chaperone complement lead to a decrease in the positive value of Δ ( C U ) T O T . Fig. 8 describes the differential effect of chaperone inclusion versus non-inclusion, on the difference in the three concentration measures considered at the heat shock and basal temperatures. Based on the fact that all of the values in Fig. 8a are positive and all of the values in Fig. 8b are negative these two figures clearly reinforce the point that, for a system undergoing heat shock, having any form of chaperone complement is better than not. Model simplicity versus biological reality In the current work we set out to model the holdase-chaperone response in a cell-like context and in doing so, identify the physical constraints necessary to define an optimal chaperone constitution in response to stress. To help advance both the sincerity of the current cause and scientific utility of these findings, here I highlight the approximations and deficiencies in the approach along with the justification for why I pursued this line of modelling (in spite of any associated limitations). In addition to pointing out the weaknesses in the argument the subsequent discussion of the approximations, along with suggestions to increase the sophistication of the simulation, provides a degree of insight into how nature may finesse these situations in practice. The most obvious approximation is that of treating the system as a well-mixed solution at equilibrium. Technically, this will never be true for a cell, in which numerous irreversible reactions are taking place and material is in dynamic flux between various compartments/sub-regions and the outside environment. However if the systems under consideration are able to relax on a time scale that is short compared with the non-equilibrium changes occurring in the cell, then the assumption of an effective instantaneous establishment of microscopic equilibrium (IEME) is applicable and that is what was done here [ 93 ]. To combat this deficiency an alternative, more complex simulation approach could have been used based on particle- or macroscopic-level kinetic and spatial modelling of the type described by Hall and Hoshino [ 94 ] or others [ 45 , 95 ]. Alternatively the well mixed approximation could have been maintained but the kinetic domain simulated as per the FoldEco model of Powers and coworkers [ 18 , 96 ] 15 . The reason such an approach was not pursued was due to current uncertainty about the interaction networks and the time constants regulating their individual association steps, the identities of the favored substrates and the relevant spatial partitioning of the chaperone networks. The use of the word uncertainty is not an intentional slur on the field as information of this type does exist to a certain extent, for instance see the screening study (and empirical analysis conducted thereof) by Taipale, Tucker and coworkers [ 15 ] along with other similar efforts [ 22 – 24 , 29 , 98 ]. Nevertheless, I would argue from a modelling perspective that such data is currently insufficient to justify more detailed approaches 16 . On the basis of the preceding argument, the well-mixed equilibrium approximation is, I think, appropriate for the current level of system definition. A further criticism that could be leveled is the description of this at-equilibrium system using expressions that are thermodynamically ideal in nature as compared to a formulation recognizing and incorporating the relevant thermodynamic non-ideality imparted by the extremely high concentrations of macromolecule considered in this study (~200–300 mg/ml of total protein) [ 89 , 90 ]. With regard to this point we note that incorporation of non-ideality into the chemical equilibria can be achieved by modifying the unfolding and affinity constants with a correction factor based on thermodynamic activity coefficients [ 56 , 99 ]. Such correction factors can be calculated to various levels of exactness using theory inclusive of both repulsive and attractive interactions [ 56 , 100 ]. That we have not done this implies that the values of K F i U i and K C j U utilized in this work should be taken as those operative in a non-ideal environment 17 . With regards to the description of the foldable protein pool, the assumption that all proteins can be considered as soluble monomers exhibiting two-state folding behavior neglects the fact that proteins can (i) exist in complexed form e.g. from simple dimers to components in huge multi-subunit biological machines [ 104 ], (ii) may be obligate membrane proteins, peripherally or integrally located, and therefore follow a temperature unfolding profile different from that exhibited by proteins normally resident in solution space [ 105 ] and (iii) may not display two-state folding behavior, typically the case as proteins become larger [ 106 ]. Recognizing that all of the preceding points are true, does not fundamentally detract from the basic veracity of our model but rather adds to it. As an example, that proteins associate to form complexes is a known mechanism for regulating and stabilizing function [ 107 ]. As noted in the preceding section, a major unappreciated aspect of the holdase mechanistic paradigm (i.e. the preferential chaperone binding to the unfolded state) is that it results in the shifting of the equilibrium towards the chaperone-unfolded state complex, thereby significantly decreasing the concentration of the folded species. To counteract this behavior I speculate as to the possible existence of a putative helper ‘H’ class chaperone that conversely binds to the folded state and thereby drags the equilibrium towards a functional complexed form ( Eqn. 14 ) 18 . [16] HF     ⇄     (H  + )     F     ⇄     U     ( + C)     ⇄     CU Interestingly, the existence of any higher-order complexation of the folded state (such as oligomerization) would act as an alternative functional executor of this role, therefore also being of benefit to the cell. Any positive temperature dependence of this association would further enhance this benefit. Further to this point extension of the mechanistic actuality of the H class of chaperone to an adsorptive surface site may extend our understanding of functional chaperones to active surfaces capable of stably maintaining a surface bound enzyme in active form [ 108 ]. Likewise a surface adsorption site may also be considered a conventional C class holdase chaperone if it preferentially bound unfolded proteins in a controllable reversible manner [ 109 ]. With regard to not specifying protein unfolding in the membrane in our simulations, this situation proved somewhat more conceptually difficult to tackle for a number of reasons relating to lack of information about chaperones specific to membrane proteins [ 110 , 111 ], and the complexity of membrane protein folding behavior due to their large sizes and tight regulation by membrane lipid composition (and the resultant phase changes determined by this composition). We will leave this as an unresolved question but with the situation all the better for having identified this untreated aspect as an open question. The final assumption inherent in our description of the foldable protein pool was the treatment of all ( N P ) TOT proteins as two-state folders. There is significant experimental and theoretical support indicating that as proteins become larger they tend to adopt a multi-domain structure [ 25 , 106 , 112 ]. As such the unfolding profile of large proteins tends to become more complex exhibiting multi-state characteristics [ 112 ]. Such a situation has interesting consequences for the holdase-chaperone mechanism of action. Holdase chaperones could bind to non-enzymatic unfolded regions of a large multi-domain protein and thereby potentially play a stabilizing role for the still functional protein as per the requirements of the putative H class of chaperones raised in the preceding section. Depending on the relative stability and functional role played by the different domain regions the behavior of such multi-state folding behavior is likely to be complex and itself ripe for further analysis. On this basis we provide the caveat that attempts to understand the role of holdase-type chaperones for large multi-domain proteins should proceed with some care 19 . The major assumptions associated with specifying the holdase chaperone complement were (i) that each condition was defined by a total concentration of soluble, stably folded monomeric chaperones, (C C ) TOT , (ii) that this total pool was divided into (N C ) TOT classes of chaperones, (iii) that each particular j th class of chaperones was defined by both their affinity for unfolded proteins (considered to be independent of the type of unfolded protein) and their total concentration, ( C C j ) TOT , and (iv) that interaction between chaperone and unfolded protein involved formation of a 1:1 complex. With regard to the first assumption that there exists a total concentration of soluble stably folded monomeric chaperones. Clearly our simulation model is deficient and should be treated as a limiting early case. Active chaperone concentrations will undoubtedly prove to be highly dynamic due to both changes in their total concentration, as well as functional regulation of chaperone activity, even outside periods of stress [ 22 , 23 ] 20 . Stress dependent induction of chaperones ( Fig. 9b ) is instigated by stress activated transcription factor promotor sequence recognition and can produce a one to eight-fold overproduction (c.f. basal condition) of the required chaperone proteins 21 [ 23 , 24 , 91 , 92 ]. When no longer required, proteases normally ascendant during the basal non-stressed condition, cleave the excess chaperone, rapidly returning its concentration to required pre-stress levels [ 116 , 117 ]. However, given that proteins unfold over a microsecond to second timescale [ 2 , 25 ] and the transcription/translation based heat shock response can take minutes [ 22 , 62 ] obviously some other forms of stress dependent control of chaperone action should also be operative [ 9 , 80 , 118 ]. Fig. 9c – g describe some potential mechanisms able to affect this control over a more immediate time range – these include allosteric regulation by a ligand (or co-chaperone) whose concentration is alterable by expression (or changeable via covalent modification by an enzyme or effector) ( Fig. 9c ) [ 119 – 121 ], stress induced desorption of chaperone from either a membranous region ( Fig. 9d [ 122 ]) or from cytoskeletal surfaces ( Fig. 9e [ 123 ]), stress induced regulation of the quaternary state of the chaperone ( Fig. 9f [ 31 , 79 , 80 , 124 ]) and direct nesting of the chaperone response within the same stress-induced production pathway of unfolded protein, such that the unfolded form of the chaperone constituted its active form ( Fig. 9g ). Although likely to be true, assumptions (ii and iii) (that there are ( N C ) TOT classes of chaperones and that each class is defined by both their non-specific affinity for unfolded proteins and total concentration, ( C C j ) TOT ), were implemented in a simplistic manner that could be refined somewhat. Chief amongst a possible array of improvements would be dismissal of the assumption of a general non-specific affinity for all types of unfolded protein ( Eqn. 6 ) [ 17 ]. As per Fig. 10a it would make biological sense if each chaperone had a limited number of target substrates with one possibility for this selection being based on proteins sharing a common stress susceptibility ( Fig. 10b ). Interestingly, such a picture bears a strong resemblance to the concept of various pH buffers used in solution biochemistry but in this instance the different holdase- chaperone types would represent a spectrum of ‘unfolding buffers’ each defined by a pK FU (T) and a buffering strength ( C C j ) TOT . The final assumption (iv) that interaction between chaperone and unfolded protein involved formation of a 1:1 complex will also not be true in all cases. Some prominent holdase-chaperone classes such as the Ibp sHsps of bacteria are known to form loose complexes 22 from which the unfolded protein may be more easily recoverable after conclusion of the stress condition [ 83 ] [ Eqn. 17 ].

Concluding remarks Many reported chaperone studies involve adding the suspected chaperone to a few different model protein systems that are capable of undergoing unfolding and aggregation. Slowing of the aggregation rate by the added chaperone candidate is frequently deemed sufficient to confer upon it holdase chaperone function. Although such types of findings are highly suggestive of chaperone function, there are lingering epistemological dangers related to definition of an adequate control and whether or not the limited number of model aggregating systems used are suitably representative catch-all targets for every holdase type chaperones. These types of questions prompted the current investigation and as such the major aim of this work was to reduce confusion (largely of the author) by providing a framework to order, and subsequently interpret, the large amount of experimental data in the chaperone biology field. In formalizing a framework for solution of the optimization question, this study has perhaps put some of the general arguments associated with holdase-chaperone action on a more quantifiable footing. Certain aspects defined in this study, such as the specification of the protein target and chaperone concentration distributions along with their stability profiles can be experimentally determined and indeed steps are being made in this direction [ 8 , 16 , 17 ]. Other features, such as the quantitative evaluation of an optimized chaperone response, likely require more careful planning of experiments prior to their implementation. In closing, my personal viewpoint of holdase-chaperone biology has evolved to considering them as a set of sophisticated detergents, exquisitely regulated by nature to help prevent the contents of the cell from precipitating. Achieving a deeper understanding of this fascinating cellular function promises advantages in many areas, particularly so for the field of medicine [ 16 , 126 ]. So called pharmacological chaperones and mini-chaperones [ 127 ] are being developed to tackle amyloidosis diseases 23 [ 128 – 130 ] along with cancer [ 131 ] and anti-aging therapies [ 132 , 133 ]. It is hoped that the current work will contribute to stimulating quantitative thinking associated with the chaperone biology field, in general, and chaperone-based pharmacotherapy strategies, in the particular. Table 1: Summary of the Major Molecular Chaperones Name Function and Structure Hsp100 (ClpB) • Function to disaggregate protein aggregates together with Hsp70 chaperones. • Members of the AAA+ ATPase superfamily. • Form two-tiered hexameric ring possessing central pore. • Well studied examples of this family include human Clpx, plant Hsp101, yeast Hsp104 and bacterial Clp [see 68 ]. Hsp90 (HtpG) • Functions to refold denatured proteins. • Role in cell signalling with particular protein substrate targets that include kinases, transcription factors and hormone receptors. • Eukaryotes have four different general types of Hsp90 defined by subcellular location (i) Cytosolic Hsp90 (α and β), (ii) Endoplasmic Reticulum Grp94, (iii) Mitochondrial TRAP1, (iv) Plastid Hsp90CP. • Hsp90 homodimer undergoes dramatic conformational changes over the course of its ATP hydrolytic to enable unfolding of tangled protein stuck in an incorrect folding state. • Prominent examples include human Hsp 90, yeast Hsp82 and bacterial HtpG. [see 69 ]. Hsp70 (DnaK) • Functions independently as a foldase and in combination with Hsp100 to disaggregate aggregates. • Role in de novo protein folding, protein transport, membrane translocation, refolding of stress denatured proteins and protein degradation. • Eukaryotes have four different general types of Hsp70 defined by subcellular location (i) Cytosolic Hsp70, (ii) Endoplasmic Reticulum ER Hsp70, (iii) Mitochondria MTHSP70, (iv) Chloroplast CP Hsp70. • Hsp70 is monomeric and consists of two structural domains – a nucleotide binding domain (NBD) capable of binding and hydrolysing ATP, and a substrate binding domain (SBD) capable of recognizing and binding to unfolded proteins in a relatively non-specific (with regards to sequence) manner. • Hsp70 acts as a switchable holdase, with the ATP bound state displaying weak affinity, and the ADP bound state displaying strong affinity, for unfolded protein substrate. • Suspected interaction with a range of co-chaperones to both regulate and recruit Hsp70 function [see 70 ]. Hsp60 (GroEL) • Functions as a foldase – Hsp60 (GroEL) is the major component of the group I chaperonin foldase machinery 1 (with the second component being Hsp10 (GroES)). • Hsp60/Hsp10 (GroEL/GroES) form a conserved double heptameric ring superstructure of ~900 MDa that creates a functional cylinder with a central cavity able to admit unfolded proteins. • Cycles of ATP hydrolysis lead to rotation of one hemispheric component of the chaperonin cylinder promoting rapid folding and unfolding of the protein substrate located within. • Release of the folded protein is achieved by uncapping the Hsp10/GroES lid. • In eukaryotes group I chaperonins are found in the mitochondria and in chloroplasts of plants [see 71 ]. sHsps • sHsps bind to unfolded proteins functioning as ATP-independent holdase-type chaperones. • The 10 sHsps found in humans are denoted as HspB1–10 with HspB4 and 5 respectively also known as αA crystallin and αB crystallin. • αA and αB crystallin are the major protein components of the eye lens (present at ~100 mg/ml) and are also ubiquitously expressed in all other tissue types. • Self-associate to form large oligomeric structures composed of 12–48 proteins with the degree of oligomerization thought to be temperature dependent. • Each sHsp protein displays a characteristic three-domain structure with unfolded N and C terminal residues and a conserved central folded alpha-crystallin domain [see 72 ]. 1 A second type of chaperonin machinery – the group II chaperonins – share a similar cylindrical superstructure (octameric instead of heptameric) and mode of action to the Hsp60/Hsp10 system. Found only in eukaryotes they are completely constructed from self-assembly of the tailless complex polypeptide-1 ring complex protein (TriC) [see 73 ].
